What Counts as a Washer and Dryer Hookup? A Renter's Guide

Published July 27, 2026

Apartment listings love the phrase "washer and dryer hookups available." It sounds simple. It is not. A hookup can mean a 240-volt outlet for an electric dryer, a gas line for a gas dryer, an exterior vent, or none of those, and the listing rarely tells you which. Renters find out what they actually have after they sign the lease, sometimes after a rental company already showed up to install.

Here is what each type of hookup actually means, so you can check before you commit.

What Is a Washer and Dryer Hookup?

A hookup is the set of utility connections a washer and dryer need to run. For a washer, that is a power outlet, hot and cold water shutoff valves, and a drain. For a dryer, that is a power outlet and, depending on the type, either an exterior vent or a gas line. A unit with hookups has this infrastructure already in place. It does not come with the machines themselves.

Landlords add hookups once, when the unit is built or renovated. They rarely change them for a new tenant. Whatever hookup type is already there is what you are working with.

Electrical Requirements: 110V vs. 220V

A washer plugs into a standard household outlet, the same 120-volt, 3-prong outlet you would use for a lamp or a microwave. Almost every laundry closet has one.

An electric dryer needs something different: a dedicated 240-volt outlet, usually a 30-amp circuit. It looks nothing like a standard outlet. Older units use a 3-prong plug (NEMA 10-30). Newer construction uses a 4-prong plug (NEMA 14-30). If your laundry closet only has a regular wall outlet, it cannot run an electric dryer without an electrician adding a 240-volt circuit, which is not a quick or cheap fix.

Gas vs. Electric Dryer Hookups

Gas dryers still need power, but only a standard 120-volt outlet for the drum motor, timer, and igniter. What they need in addition is a gas line, natural gas or propane, with a shutoff valve within reach of the machine. Gas hookups are common in single-family homes and less common in apartments, since running a gas line into a multi-unit building costs more.

Electric dryers skip the gas line entirely but need that 240-volt outlet instead. Neither type is universally better. The type that matters is whichever one your unit already has, because retrofitting the other requires a licensed electrician or plumber and your landlord's approval.

Ask directly: "Is the dryer hookup gas or electric?" Many listings just say "hookups available" and leave out this detail entirely.

Vented vs. Ventless Dryers

A standard vented dryer pushes hot, moist air out through a duct to the outside of the building. This is what most single-family homes and ground-floor apartments have.

A ventless dryer, sometimes called a condenser or heat-pump dryer, does not need an exterior duct at all. It condenses moisture out of the air and either collects it in a removable reservoir or routes it into a drain line. Ventless dryers show up in interior laundry closets, upper-floor units, and older buildings where running a duct to the outside was never possible.

If your laundry closet has no visible duct running to an exterior wall, you likely need a ventless-compatible dryer, not a standard vented one. Mixing the two up means a dryer that either cannot be installed or runs with nowhere for the exhaust to go.

Water Supply and Drain Lines

Washers need three more connections beyond power: a hot water shutoff valve, a cold water shutoff valve, and a drain. The valves are usually two knobs on the wall behind where the washer sits. The drain is either a standpipe, an open vertical pipe set 30 to 42 inches above the floor, or a floor drain nearby. Some newer units route the drain hose into a laundry sink instead.

If any of these three are missing, water has nowhere to go in, or nowhere to go out, and a washer cannot be installed until a plumber adds them.

A Quick Checklist Before You Sign a Lease

  • Is there a dedicated laundry closet or area with visible hookups, not just floor space?
  • Is the outlet a standard 120V plug (washer only) or a 240V outlet (needed for an electric dryer)?
  • Is there a gas line and shutoff valve near the dryer spot, or no gas line at all?
  • Is there a duct running to an exterior wall, or is the closet fully interior?
  • Are there hot and cold water valves and a drain standpipe behind the washer spot?
  • Ask the landlord or property manager directly which type is installed. Do not assume from the listing text alone.

What If Your Unit Has No Hookups at All?

Some units genuinely have no laundry infrastructure: no outlet built for a dryer, no water valves, no drain. Adding all of that from scratch means cutting into walls, running new plumbing, and getting your landlord's sign-off. That is rarely worth it for a rental you might leave in a year or two. A community laundry room or laundromat is the realistic option here, not a retrofit.

But "no hookups" in a listing sometimes just means "no machines installed," not "no infrastructure." It is worth asking the landlord directly which one they mean before you rule out in-unit laundry entirely.
